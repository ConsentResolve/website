## The lead just came in — now what?

Picture the moment most owners actually live with the TCPA. A new lead lands in your inbox or your CRM. You've got a phone, a thumb, and ten free minutes. The question that flashes through your head is the practical one: *Can I just text this person? Should I call?* That small decision, made a dozen times a week, is exactly where home-service shops get themselves into trouble — not because owners are reckless, but because nobody ever gave them a straight rule.

Here it is in plain English. The TCPA — the Telephone Consumer Protection Act — is the federal law that restricts unsolicited marketing calls and texts to consumers. It decides whether that text you're about to send is a normal follow-up or a legal liability. And the whole thing turns on one word: consent.

## Why the decision feels riskier than it should

The reason that "should I text them?" moment is stressful is that the downside is wildly out of proportion to the action. Sending one text takes two seconds. But the TCPA carries statutory damages of [$500 to $1,500 per unsolicited call or text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/227), and it includes a private right of action — meaning the person on the other end can sue you directly, per message, with no regulator involved.

So the math an owner has to do in that moment is brutal. Text a hundred leads you weren't sure about, and you're not looking at a warning — you're looking at arithmetic that can end in a five- or six-figure settlement. These aren't predictions about your shop. They're the reason it's worth setting up your follow-up so the question "is this safe?" never has to be asked one lead at a time.

And the TCPA doesn't sit alone. The cost of getting [consent](/resources/glossary/#consent) wrong shows up everywhere regulators look. California's wiretap statute, CIPA, carries [$5,000 per violation](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=637.2) and is now mass-filed against websites that track visitors without permission. Texas secured a [$1.4 billion settlement with Meta](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-secures-14-billion-settlement-meta-over-its-unauthorized-capture) over biometric data captured without consent, and a [$1.375 billion settlement with Google](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-secures-historic-1375-billion-settlement-google-related-texans-data) over tracking Texans without consent. In January 2025, Texas filed the [first-ever enforcement suit](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-sues-allstate-and-arity-unlawfully-collecting-using-and-selling-over-45) under its comprehensive data-privacy law, the TDPSA — again, over data collected and sold without consent. The pattern is the same every time: the penalty wasn't for marketing, it was for marketing to people who never said yes.

## What's actually safe, and what isn't

Strip the TCPA down to what an owner needs at the keyboard and it's two questions. Did this person clearly agree to hear from me? And can I prove it? If the answer to both is yes, reaching out is on solid ground. If either answer is "I think so," you're guessing — and guessing is the expensive part.

That's why a [timestamped consent log](/features/built-in-compliance/) isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's the thing that converts your gut-check into a fact. Instead of squinting at a lead and wondering where it came from, you know: this person accepted a clear banner on your site at a specific time, and the record is attached. The decision stops being a judgment call and becomes a lookup.

## Is buying a lead list ever the safe move?

Owners ask this constantly, because a list looks like a shortcut. The honest answer is that a purchased or shared list is the one place the "is this safe to contact?" question gets hardest to answer — because the consent, if any, belongs to someone else.

There's a lesson buried in enforcement history that has nothing to do with your dialing finger. When the FTC ordered Angi's HomeAdvisor to pay a [$7.2 million settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-order-requires-homeadvisor-pay-72-million-stop-deceptively-marketing-its-leads-home-improvement) — over $3 million of it refunded to pros — it was about deceptive claims around lead quality and source. Where your leads come from, and whether the people behind them actually consented, isn't a detail you can outsource. An [exclusive, consent-first lead](/why-consent-first/) comes with a record you own. A bought list comes with someone else's assumptions and your liability.

## The rule that makes the decision disappear

Here's how owners stay safe without going quiet or overthinking every follow-up:

- **Only contact people who said yes.** Identify visitors who accept a clear banner. No banner, no contact — the risky leads never enter your pipeline in the first place.
