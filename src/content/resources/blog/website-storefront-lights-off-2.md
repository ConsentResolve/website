## A store full of customers you can't see

Picture this. You own a shop on Main Street. The door's open, the sign's lit, and people walk in all day long. They browse the shelves, pick things up, compare two options, nod to themselves — and walk out. Now picture doing all of that with the lights off. You can hear footsteps. You know people are in there. But you can't see a single face, can't say hello, can't answer a question, can't ring anyone up.

That's your website right now. Visitors come in every day. You just can't see them.

## The problem with a dark storefront

Here's how dark it actually is. Across home-service sites, about [98% of visitors leave without ever converting or identifying themselves](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/conversion-rate-benchmarks). And they don't linger — the average visit runs roughly [87 seconds](https://spectruminfinite.com/blogs/average-time-spent-on-website-2025/) before they're gone. You can see both figures, sourced, on our [stats page](/stats/).

So the typical homeowner walks into your dark storefront, spends a minute and a half feeling around in the dark, finds *most* of what they need, and leaves. They didn't decide against you. They just couldn't be served, because you couldn't see them. And here's the kicker: you *paid* to get them through the door — in ad spend, in SEO work, in the hours you put into the site. The visit happened. The light was off.

## So why isn't more traffic the answer?

Because more visitors to a dark store just means more footsteps you can't see. This is the trap a lot of contractors fall into — they assume a slow phone means they need more clicks, so they pour money into ads to drag more strangers through the door of a shop where the lights are still off. The bucket leaks just as fast; there's simply more pouring in the top.

The problem was never the amount of traffic. It's that you can't see the traffic you've already got. Flip the switch first, and *then* deciding whether you need more makes sense. (If you're weighing that, it's worth seeing [how each lead channel really costs out](/resources/compare/) before you spend more.)

## Turning the lights on

Turning on the lights means [visitor identification](/resources/glossary/#visitor-identification) — done the consent-first way. When a visitor accepts a clear consent banner, that anonymous footstep becomes a face: a real name and a consented email, logged with a timestamp. Now you can do what any shopkeeper would — follow up, answer the question they were quietly asking, invite them to book.

The "consent-first" part matters, because the old way of secretly watching everyone in the store is exactly what's landing companies in privacy lawsuits. Lights-on doesn't mean spying. It means greeting the people who said it's okay to be greeted. Every lead carries a signed receipt and a timestamped consent log — protection for your shop. It's exclusive to you, flat $7, and never resold to the contractor across town. [That's what visitor identification does](/features/visitor-identification/).

And once you can see them, speed wins. The first shop to follow up usually books the job, so a same-day email beats a polished one that goes out next week.

## Flip the switch this week

- **Turn on consent-first identification.** Stop letting visitors browse your storefront in the dark and walk out unseen.
- **Have a warm follow-up ready.** A short, friendly email — "saw you stopped by, want us to help?" — sent the same day.
- **Be first.** The fastest follow-up usually wins the job, so build it into your daily routine.

You don't need more people walking in. You need to see the ones already inside. Turn the lights on, and your storefront finally works the way you always thought it did.
