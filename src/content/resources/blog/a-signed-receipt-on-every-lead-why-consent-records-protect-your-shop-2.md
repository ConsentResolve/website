## The question you hope nobody ever asks

Picture the email you never want to get: a homeowner, or worse a lawyer, asking "where did you get my information?" For most contractors who buy leads, that question is a cold sweat. They don't actually know. The list came from a broker, the broker got it from somewhere, and the trail goes dark two hops back.

That's the moment a consent record exists for. Not to make you feel compliant on a normal Tuesday — to give you a clean, complete answer on the one day someone asks.

## The problem with leads that can't show their work

A lead with no record behind it is a liability wearing a customer's face. You can't prove the person agreed to anything. You can't show when, or what they agreed to, or where the lead came from. If it's ever questioned, you've got a name and a shrug.

That's the structural flaw in bought lists and shared leads: the consent — if it ever existed — lives with whoever collected it, not with you. The proof and the lead got separated somewhere upstream, and you're holding the half without the receipt.

I've seen contractors assume the broker is holding the proof for them, like a warranty card in a drawer somewhere. They aren't. Even if a broker did log consent, that record is theirs, in their system, on their terms — not something you can produce on demand when a homeowner emails *you*. By the time you need it, the relationship that could have handed it to you is three sales removed. You're left explaining a lead you can't actually account for.

## What's actually in the record?

A [consent record](/resources/glossary/#consent) is small, and that's the point — it captures exactly the three facts a challenge cares about and nothing it doesn't.

- **The timestamp.** The precise moment consent was given, down to the second. Not "sometime in spring." The exact event.
- **The consent string.** The actual banner text the visitor saw and the choice they made. This is the part most systems skip and the part that matters most — it proves not just *that* they said yes, but *what* they said yes to.
- **The source.** Where the lead originated — which site, which entry point. So the trail starts at a known place instead of a broker's "trust me."

Three fields. Who agreed, when, and to what. Every question a challenge can throw at you is answered by one of them.

People sometimes ask why the consent string matters so much when the timestamp already proves someone clicked. Here's why: "they accepted something" and "they accepted *this*" are very different claims. The consent string pins down the exact wording the visitor saw — what they were told would happen if they said yes. Without it, you can prove a click but not what the click meant. With it, there's nothing to argue about; the agreement and its terms are the same record.

## How would you actually produce it?

This is the part I get asked about most, so let me be concrete. You don't reconstruct the record. You don't dig through server logs at 11pm. The record is written the instant the visitor accepts the banner — same event as the opt-in — and it's tied to that specific lead from then on.

So producing it is a lookup. You find the lead; the lead carries its record; you hand over the timestamp, the consent string, and the source. Done. And it stays available for a seven-year audit window, because the day someone asks is rarely the day the lead came in.

That's the difference between [consent-first identification](/features/consent-first-identification/) and everything that came before it. The proof isn't filed separately and hoped for later. The lead *is* the proof. They're the same object, written together, kept together.

## Why this protects the shop, not just the lawyer

Privacy enforcement isn't theoretical anymore — states are bringing real cases over data collected and used without consent. The point of a record isn't fear; it's that you simply opt out of the whole problem. You can't be the shop that mishandled consent if every lead you touched arrived with its consent already proven.

That's what a signed receipt on every lead actually buys: not a feeling of compliance, but a finished answer sitting in a file, ready before the question is ever asked. You follow up by email, into your own funnel, and if anyone ever wants to see the paperwork — you have it.

And the seven-year window matters more than it sounds. The gap between when a lead comes in and when anyone questions it can be years — a homeowner who forgot they ever visited your site, a regulator working through a backlog. Memory fades; a stored record doesn't. The reason to keep it that long isn't that you expect trouble on most leads. It's that you don't get to choose which lead becomes the one someone asks about, so every lead carries the same receipt by default.

## What to take away

- **The record is three facts:** timestamp, consent string, source. Memorize those — they're the whole answer to any challenge.
- **It's automatic.** You don't build it or file it; it's written the moment the visitor accepts.
- **Producing it is a lookup,** not a scramble, for seven years.
- **The lead and the proof are one thing.** That's the structural advantage no bought list can match.

When the question finally comes — and for someone, someday, it will — you want the answer to be a receipt, not a shrug. Every consent-first lead already carries one.
