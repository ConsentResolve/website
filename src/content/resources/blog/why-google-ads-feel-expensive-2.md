## The bill that doesn't add up

Every contractor running Google Ads has had this moment: the monthly invoice lands, and it stings. The instinct is to blame the obvious thing — the click costs too much. Clicks are pricey, sure. But the click is almost never why your ads *feel* expensive.

The click did its job. It got a homeowner onto your site. The expense isn't in the hello — it's in everything that happens, or fails to happen, after it.

## Where the money actually goes

Let's talk real numbers. Google's Local Services Ads run about [$53 per lead on a blended average](https://searchlightdigital.io/google-local-service-ads-cost-per-lead/), and it climbs from there by trade: [HVAC $45–$85, Plumbing $35–$65, Electrical $35–$70, Roofing $50–$95](https://www.homeservicedirect.net/local-service-ads-for-contractors/). You can see those figures sourced on our [stats page](/stats/).

Now sit with that. Fifty-some dollars to put one interested homeowner in front of your business. That's not a crazy price *if the visit turns into a job*. The trouble is what happens next: most of those expensive visitors look around, don't book, and close the tab. You paid full freight for the click — and got nothing back.

Run the math across a month. If you're paying for a hundred visits and a handful book, you didn't pay $53 a lead. You paid $53 times a hundred, divided by the few jobs you actually closed. *That's* your real cost per job, and it's a multiple of the click price. The click was cheap. The waste was expensive.

## Why is my cost per job so much higher than my cost per click?

Because a [cost per lead](/resources/glossary/#cost-per-lead) and a cost per *job* are two completely different animals, and contractors quietly confuse them all the time.

A click buys you a conversation starter. A job requires the conversation to continue — and for most home-service shops, it never does. The homeowner who priced your service at 8 p.m. on a Tuesday didn't decide against you. They got distracted, meant to call, and forgot. You paid to bring them in and then had no way to bring them back.

That gap — between the visit you paid for and the follow-up that never came — is the single most expensive thing in your ad account. Not the click. The silence after it.

## The cheaper lever nobody pulls

Here's the part that flips the whole problem: you've already paid the expensive part. The visitor is on your site. Bringing them *back* costs a fraction of bringing in a new one.

When a homeowner accepts a clear consent banner, [consent-first identification](/resources/glossary/#consent-first) turns that anonymous visit into a real, exclusive contact — flat $7, never resold, email-grade so you follow up by email, never a cold call. Compare that to paying another $53-plus to attract a brand-new stranger who'll probably also leave. You're not buying more clicks. You're finally finishing the ones you bought.

The first shop to follow up usually wins the job, too — so capturing the visitor isn't enough on its own; getting back to them fast is what closes the gap. Before you pour more into ads, it's worth seeing [how each lead channel really stacks up on cost](/resources/compare/).

## What to do before your next ad spend

- **Stop judging ads by the click.** Track cost per *job* instead — visits divided by booked work. That number is the truth.
- **Capture the visitors you already pay for.** Turn on consent-first identification so the homeowners who don't call become exclusive leads instead of vanishing.
- **Follow up first.** A fast, friendly email to a recovered visitor turns expensive traffic into booked jobs — and spreads your ad spend across more of them.

Your Google Ads probably aren't overpriced. They're under-finished. Plug the leak after the click, and the same budget suddenly looks a whole lot cheaper.
