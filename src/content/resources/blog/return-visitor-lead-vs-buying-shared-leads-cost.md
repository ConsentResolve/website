## You're paying twice and only counting once

Here's a pattern I see in almost every business's numbers. They spend real money buying leads from a platform — a few hundred, sometimes a few thousand a month. And separately, they spend real money getting visitors to their website through ads, signage, their Google listing, word of mouth. Two budgets, both working.

Then the visitor who came to the website leaves without calling — [98% of visitors do](/stats/) — and later comes *back* for a second look. That return visit is the warmest lead the business will see all week. And most businesses let it go by for nothing while they keep writing checks for colder, shared leads somewhere else. You paid to bring that visitor in. When they come back, the smart move is to recover them, not ignore them and buy a stranger instead.

## What a shared lead actually costs you

Let's put real numbers on the alternative. A [shared lead](/resources/glossary/#shared-lead) on the big platforms isn't cheap, and it isn't yours. Shared-lead marketplaces run around $46 to $50 per lead loaded across industries. Those are the stickers. The catch is baked into the model: the same visitor gets sold to four or five businesses at once.

So play out a month. Say you buy ten shared leads at $50 each — $500 spent. They're split among five businesses, everyone calls, and realistically you book two. Your cost per *lead* was $50. Your cost per *closed deal* was $250. And you earned those two jobs by winning a footrace against four other businesses, which means you were also the one eating the phone time and quotes on the eight that went nowhere.

That's the honest price of a shared lead: full freight for a lead you have to fight four competitors to keep.

## What the returning visitor costs instead

Now the other side. A visitor who accepted your consent banner on their first visit is a known, opted-in contact. When they come back, you can recover them — send a timely, helpful email into the funnel you already run — at custom pricing, exclusive to you and never resold.

Compare the two on the number that matters. Even if only, say, one in three of your recovered returning visitors books — a conservative rate for someone who already found you, considered you, and came back on their own — your lead cost per closed deal stays a small fraction of the shared lead's $250. That's not a rounding difference. That's the gap between a channel that pays and one that bleeds.

And the returning visitor has two structural advantages a shared lead never will:

- **Exclusivity.** Nobody else got this lead. You're not the fifth call the visitor fields today; you're the business they already came back to. Your book rate isn't capped by four competitors dialing faster.
- **Warmth.** A shared lead is a stranger who filled out a form on someone else's site. A returning visitor chose *your* site, twice. They've seen your work and your reviews. Half the trust-building is already done before you send a word.

## Why speed makes the cheap lead even cheaper

The returning-visitor lead gets better still when you act fast, and this is where the economics compound. [78% of visitors hire the business who responds first](https://www.vendasta.com/blog/lead-response-time/) — not the cheapest, not the highest-rated, the fastest. On a shared lead, "first" is nearly impossible: four other businesses got the same alert at the same second, so speed is a coin flip you're paying $50 to enter.

On a returning-visitor lead, *you're the only one who got the signal.* Being first isn't a race — it's a given, because nobody else even knows the visitor came back. So the one factor that decides most jobs is handed to you for free, on top of the already-low cost of the lead. The shared lead makes you buy your way into a speed contest. The returning visitor makes you the only runner.

## Where the returning visitor doesn't replace everything

To be fair about it: recovering returning visitors isn't a total substitute for every other channel, and it doesn't pretend to be. It only works on traffic you already have. If a visitor never found your site in the first place, there's no visit to recover — which is why ads, your Google listing, and yes, sometimes a platform, still have a job to do at the top of the funnel filling the pipe.

The mistake isn't buying leads. It's buying *cold* leads to chase visitors while ignoring the warm ones already circling back. A returning visitor and a Google Local Services lead aren't really competitors; LSA brings you exclusive contact from someone actively searching, and a returning-visitor lead recovers someone you already attracted. What you want to cut is the expensive, low-odds middle — the shared lead sold five ways that costs $50 and books like a coin flip. Recover your returning visitors first, keep the channels that give you exclusive contact, and let the shared platforms compete for whatever's left on the honest cost-per-job number.

## The comparison businesses never run

The reason this gap stays invisible is that the two channels get measured differently. The shared lead shows up as a line item — a clear invoice, easy to point to. The returning visitor you missed shows up as nothing at all, because you never knew it happened. You can't put "the warm lead I didn't recover" on a spreadsheet, so it never enters the comparison, and the business keeps funding the expensive channel while the cheap one goes to waste.

Once you make the returning visit visible — turn it into a recoverable, consented lead — the math corrects itself. You start comparing sources on cost per *closed deal*, and the exclusive returning visitor almost always wins against a $50 lead split five ways. Not because the price is a magic number, but because you stopped paying full price to fight four competitors for a stranger when a visitor who already likes you was standing at the door.

## Run the number on your own business

You don't have to take the example on faith — the comparison works with your real figures. Pull two numbers from your CRM: how much you spent on your current lead source last quarter, and how many jobs it actually produced. Divide the first by the second and you have your cost per closed deal on that channel. Now count how many visitors came back to your site in the same window without ever being recovered. Even a conservative book rate on those returning visitors, at custom, low per-lead pricing, almost always lands under the cost-per-job number you just calculated.

That's the exercise almost no business runs, because one side of it is invisible until you make it visible. The invoice from the platform is right there in your inbox every month; the warm lead you let walk never shows up anywhere. Turn the returning visit into a recoverable, consented lead and both sides finally sit on the same spreadsheet — and once they do, the cheaper, higher-converting channel is obvious. You stop paying premium prices to fight four competitors for strangers when visitors who already like you are knocking twice.
