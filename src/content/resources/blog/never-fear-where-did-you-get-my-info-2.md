## The five words that shouldn't rattle you

You send a friendly follow-up email to a homeowner who was just on your site pricing a job, and the reply lands: "Where did you get my info?" For a lot of pros, that's the moment the stomach drops. But the drop isn't really about the question — it's about not having rehearsed the answer. Fix that one thing and the whole exchange goes from threatening to routine.

So let's treat this the way you'd treat any tricky customer moment: with a script you've said before and proof you can point to. Done right, "where did you get my info?" isn't a confrontation. It's a 15-second non-event that often leaves the homeowner trusting you more than before they asked.

## Why the question feels like an accusation

When a contractor freezes at that question, it's almost always because the lead came from somewhere they can't cleanly explain — a purchased list, a shared platform, a vague "they were interested." You inherited someone else's [consent](/resources/glossary/#consent) assumptions, and now you're holding the bag with nothing concrete to say.

That's not just awkward; it's the exact situation privacy law is built around. The TCPA carries [$500 to $1,500 per unsolicited call or text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/227) and lets recipients sue directly. California's CIPA carries [$5,000 per violation](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=637.2) and is mass-filed against non-consented website tracking. The FTC ordered Angi's HomeAdvisor to pay a [$7.2 million settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-order-requires-homeadvisor-pay-72-million-stop-deceptively-marketing-its-leads-home-improvement) over deceptive claims about where its leads came from. None of those are forecasts for your shop — they're the reason it pays to know exactly where every contact came from before anyone asks.

## The script — say it like this

Here's the actual reply, short enough to say without thinking:

*"You visited our website and accepted our consent banner — it's logged with the date and time you opted in. Want me to read you the exact timestamp?"*

That's the whole thing. Notice what it does. It's calm, it's specific, and it offers more proof rather than backing away from it. You're not apologizing and you're not getting defensive — you're reading back a fact. Nine times out of ten the homeowner says "oh, right, that makes sense," and you're back to talking about their roof or their AC.

Compare that to the version without a record: *"Uh, I'm not totally sure — we work with a few lead sources."* Same information, opposite outcome. The first answer makes you sound like a professional who keeps clean records. The second makes a curious homeowner suspicious. The difference isn't your personality on a given day. It's whether you have the receipt.
