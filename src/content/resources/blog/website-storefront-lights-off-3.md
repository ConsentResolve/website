## Walk down Main Street in the dark

Imagine a shop on Main Street with the lights off. The door's unlocked, the products are on the shelves, but inside it's pitch black. People wander in, squint around, can't find the counter or anyone to help, and within a minute they walk back out. The owner never even knows they were there.

That's most contractor websites. Not because the work is bad or the site is ugly — but because the lights are off. Visitors come in, look around, and leave, and you have no idea who they were or how to reach them.

## The traffic showed up. You just can't see it.

The cruel part is the foot traffic is real. You paid for it — in ad spend, in the hours you put into ranking. People are clicking through that door. But the average visitor spends about [87 seconds](https://spectruminfinite.com/blogs/average-time-spent-on-website-2025/) on a site before moving on, and across home-service websites roughly [98% of visitors never convert or identify themselves](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/conversion-rate-benchmarks).

So the shopper who would have happily talked to you slips out in the dark. You don't get a name, an email, or a second chance. The contact form catches the rare person willing to fill it out — and waves goodbye to everyone else.

And notice it's not the bad-fit visitors leaving. The person who clicked through, read your reviews, and scoped your service area was *interested.* They were the warm one. A storefront with the lights on greets that person, learns their name, and points them to the counter. A dark one lets your warmest prospect of the day wander out without ever knowing they came. The form-fillers are the few who liked you enough to fumble through your contact fields in the dark. Everyone else — the polite, the busy, the merely cautious — just leaves.

## What does a dark storefront actually cost?

Here's where it stops being a metaphor and starts being money. Every anonymous visitor is foot traffic you already paid to bring through the door. When they leave without a trace, you've spent the acquisition cost and gotten exactly nothing in return — and worse, one of those strangers is likely to book the competitor down the street who simply followed up.

Run it for your own shop. If a hundred people visit this month and your form catches a couple, the other ninety-eight walked in and out in the dark. Whatever you paid to get them there, you paid it for ninety-eight ghosts. That's not a marketing rounding error. That's jobs — real installs, real repairs, real revenue — leaking out a door you can't see.

## Flipping the switch

Turning the lights on means [visitor identification](/resources/glossary/#visitor-identification), done the consent-first way. When a visitor lands on your site and accepts a clear consent banner, [Consent Resolve](/features/visitor-identification/) turns that anonymous, *consenting* visitor into a real contact: a name and a consented email, logged with a timestamp. No form fill, and no phone number to cold-call — follow-up is email, into the funnel you already run.

Suddenly you can see who walked in. The homeowner who browsed your install gallery for eighty-seven seconds and left? You can send one helpful email that afternoon, while they're still deciding. The storefront isn't dark anymore.

The lights stay metaphorical, but the effect is literal: a stranger becomes a contact, a contact becomes a follow-up, and a follow-up becomes a booked job. Nothing about the homeowner's experience changes except that the banner they clicked "accept" on is what gives you permission to reach back out. They opted in. You're not chasing anyone down — you're answering someone who already showed interest, which is the easiest sale in the trade.

## Why this beats buying more foot traffic

The instinct when sales are slow is to drive more people through the door — more ads, more SEO, more spend. But pouring traffic into a dark storefront just means more people leave without buying. You're scaling the leak.

Turning on the lights does the opposite: it pulls more booked jobs out of the foot traffic you *already* have. And the price makes it easy. Recovering a visitor who was already on your site costs a flat **$7**, and that lead is exclusive to you — never resold to three competitors. - **Switch on consent-first identification** so visitors stop walking out anonymous.
- **Have one same-day email ready** — short and friendly — so a recovered visitor hears from you while their project is still on their mind.
