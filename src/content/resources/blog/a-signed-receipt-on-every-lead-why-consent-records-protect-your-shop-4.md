## The question every shop eventually hears

You send a friendly follow-up email to a homeowner who was pricing a job on your site. Most reply or ignore it. But every so often, one writes back with a single line: "How did you get my information?"

It's a fair question. And in that moment, your answer is everything. If you fumble — "uh, I think you filled something out?" — you sound evasive, even though you did nothing wrong. A confused homeowner who hears a guess walks away more suspicious than when they started.

## Why a guess is the worst possible answer

The problem isn't that you broke a rule. The problem is that without a record, you can't *prove* you didn't. Memory isn't evidence. "We must have gotten consent" is not the same as showing it, and a homeowner — or worse, the regulator they complain to — can tell the difference.

When you can't point to anything specific, a routine question curdles into a dispute. The homeowner assumes the worst. You spend an afternoon trying to reconstruct a visit from weeks ago. And if it escalates, you're defending yourself with a story instead of a fact. That's the real exposure: not what you did, but that you can't show what you did.

## How does a consent receipt actually answer a homeowner — or a complaint?

This is the whole point of a [consent record](/resources/glossary/#consent-record). When a visitor accepts your banner, [Consent Resolve](/features/consent-first-identification/) writes a timestamped receipt: this person agreed to be identified and contacted, on this date, at this time. That receipt is a signed fact, not a hunch.

So when the "how did you get my info" email lands, you don't scramble. You reply with the truth, backed by the record: you visited the site, accepted the consent banner, and that agreement was logged at a specific moment. A specific, calm, factual answer settles almost every honest question — because it sounds exactly like what it is: a real business that kept its receipts.

## Why this protects the shop, not just the lawyer

The same receipt that calms a curious homeowner also stands up if the question becomes a formal complaint. Because every lead from [consent-first identification](/features/consent-first-identification/) carries its own timestamped record on a multi-year audit trail, you're never reconstructing — you're producing. A regulator's question gets the same clean answer the homeowner got.

That's why consent-first is [protection](/why-consent-first/), not red tape. It isn't about fearing a lawsuit; it's about being the shop that can answer the question every time, in seconds, with a fact. The receipt does the defending so you can stay focused on the job.

## What to put in place

- **Make every lead carry its own receipt.** Don't rely on memory or a general policy — the protection is in a per-lead, timestamped record.
- **Know your one-line answer.** Practice it: "You visited our site and accepted the consent banner on [date]; we logged it." Calm and specific beats long and defensive.
- **Keep the record reachable.** A receipt you can't find isn't a receipt. Make sure any team member who handles follow-up can produce it.
- **Treat the question as normal.** Most "how did you get my info" notes are honest, not hostile. A factual answer usually ends it right there.

A homeowner's question doesn't have to be a problem. With a signed receipt on every lead, it's just a question you can answer — clearly, instantly, and in a way that makes your shop look more trustworthy, not less.
