## Two leads, same name field, nothing alike

Open your lead list and you'll see two rows that look identical: a name, an email, a "visited your site" tag. But one of them read your full-replacement page twice, hit financing, and came back this morning. The other glanced at your homepage for fifteen seconds three weeks ago and never returned.

On a flat list, those two get the same treatment — usually whoever sits higher on the screen. That's the mistake that quietly costs jobs. The ready buyer waits behind the idle one, and by the time you reach them, someone faster already did.

## The problem isn't your effort, it's your order

Most contractors aren't lazy about follow-up; they're working the list in the wrong sequence. When every lead looks the same, you process them in arrival order, or alphabetical order, or whatever order your phone shows — none of which has anything to do with who's about to book.

That's the leaky bucket hiding in your CRM. The leads are in there. The intent is in there. But without a way to tell ready from idle, your best hours get spread evenly across people whose readiness is anything but even — and the hottest ones cool off waiting their turn.

## How do you tell a hot lead from a browser?

You read the signals the homeowner already left. That's what [lead scoring](/resources/glossary/#lead-scoring) does with the data behind a consent-first lead, and three signals carry most of the weight:

- **Page intent.** Which pages did they actually open? Pricing, financing, and a specific high-value service page say "shopping to buy." A homepage skim says "early."
- **Recency.** Were they on your site today, or a month ago? Intent decays fast; a recent visit is worth far more than an old one.
- **Return visits.** One visit is curiosity. Two or three is a homeowner working toward a decision and circling back to your name.

[Consent Resolve](/features/lead-scoring/) weighs those signals on every recovered lead — each one from a visitor who accepted a clear consent banner, with a timestamped record — so the list sorts itself by readiness instead of by accident. The deeper read on what those pages mean is its own play; we cover it in [reading a homeowner's browse trail](/resources/blog/read-the-trail-knowing-what-a-homeowner-looked-at-before-they-call/).

## A triage rule you can run on day one

You don't need a scoring spreadsheet. Stack the three signals into a rule a busy shop can actually follow:

- **Hot** = high-intent pages **plus** a recent or repeat visit. Work it first, today, by email. This is where the job is.
- **Warm** = real interest but older, or a single visit with no return. Give it a timed follow-up — a day or two out — rather than your first slot.
- **Just browsing** = a quick skim, no high-intent pages, no return. Drop it into nurture so it stays in front of them without eating your best hours.

Run that rule top to bottom and your day reorders itself around the homeowners closest to booking. The browsing leads aren't ignored — they're just not stealing the minutes that belong to the hot ones. If idle leads have been swallowing your time, the same logic drives [stopping tire-kickers from eating your day](/resources/blog/stop-tire-kickers-eating-time/).

## Why working the hot ones first actually wins

The whole point of sorting is to be fast where speed pays. [78% of homeowners hire the contractor who responds first](https://www.vendasta.com/blog/lead-response-time/) — not the cheapest, not the highest-rated, the fastest. And classic lead-response research found that reaching a fresh lead within five minutes makes you up to [21× more likely to qualify it](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf) than waiting past thirty. That 21× is about contact speed, not a promise of jobs booked — but it tells you exactly why the hot lead belongs at the top: the advantage of being first lives in the first few minutes. Both figures are sourced on our [stats page](/stats/).

You can't be first to everyone at once. Scoring decides who you're first to.
