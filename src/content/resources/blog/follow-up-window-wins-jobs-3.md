## The clock you can't see

A homeowner is on your site at 8:40 on a weeknight, pricing the work, comparing you to two others. By 8:42 they've closed the laptop. The intent that was burning hot at 8:40? It starts cooling the second they walk away from the screen — and most contractors never even know the clock started.

That stretch right after a homeowner shows interest is the follow-up window. It's where jobs are won and lost, and almost nobody on the trade side is watching it.

## Why timing beats almost everything

After years of talking to contractors, I'm convinced timing decides more jobs than price or reviews do. Here's the evidence. Contacting a lead within five minutes makes you up to [21× more likely to qualify it](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf) than waiting until after thirty. That's not a small edge — it's the difference between a conversation and a voicemail nobody returns.

And it stacks with how homeowners actually choose: about [78% hire the contractor who responds first](https://pipelineon.com/blog/how-much-does-thumbtack-charge-per-lead/). So the window isn't just about catching attention — it's about being the first voice in the room while they're still deciding. Get there inside the window and the job is often yours before the other shops have even seen the lead.

## What does the follow-up window actually look like?

Picture a curve that starts high and drops fast. The first five minutes after a homeowner browses, their intent is at its peak — they're in the mindset, the problem is front of mind, they want it handled. Every minute after that, the curve falls. By the next morning, they may have already booked someone, or talked themselves out of the project entirely.

The job isn't lost on quality. It's lost on the slope of that curve. Which means the entire game is reaching the homeowner before the curve falls off — and doing it for every interested visitor, not just the handful who happened to call.

## Hitting the window without living on the phone

Here's the catch: a person can't reliably hit a five-minute window. You're on a job, asleep, or with family at 8:40 on a weeknight. That's not a discipline problem — it's a physics problem. Manual follow-up will always be late for somebody.

No phone number to cold-call; it's email into the funnel you already run. ## The window isn't one moment — it's a few

It helps to stop picturing the follow-up window as a single five-minute dash and start picturing it as a short series of touches. The first touch, inside those first minutes, lands while intent is at its peak — that's the one that does most of the work. But homeowners are busy too. Some see your email an hour later, some the next morning. A second, gentle touch a day or two out catches the ones who were genuinely interested but got pulled away by life.

The trick is that this only works if the timing is consistent, and consistency is exactly what a human juggling job sites can't promise. A sequence — first touch fast, a polite follow-up shortly after — runs the same way every time when it's automated. You're not pestering anyone; you're being reliably present during the stretch when the homeowner is actually choosing. Most jobs that feel like they were "won on relationship" were really won by showing up, helpfully, at the right moments.

## How fast does the window really close?

Fast enough that "I'll call them back after this job" is usually too slow. The five-minute-versus-thirty-minute gap isn't a rounding error — it's a [21×](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf) swing in your odds of qualifying the lead. That tells you the curve is steep right at the start, where every minute counts the most.

The practical takeaway isn't "answer everything in five minutes" — you can't, and you shouldn't try to live that way. It's "make sure *something* useful happens in five minutes even when you're unavailable." Let automation own the instant first touch so you hit the window every time, then let your human judgment take over once the conversation is live. That split — machine handles speed, you handle the close — is how a busy shop wins jobs on timing without chaining anyone to a phone. And because every contact is a consenting visitor with a timestamped record, the speed never comes at the cost of staying on the right side of the line.

## What to do this week

- **Map your real response time.** From the moment a homeowner shows interest to your first touch — most shops are shocked how long it is.
- **Automate the first move.** Turn on consent-first follow-up so consenting visitors hear from you inside the window, hands-free.
- **Make the first touch useful.** A short, helpful email — "want us to put that quote together?" — beats a generic auto-reply.
- **Let people close, let automation start.** Speed buys you the conversation; your craft closes it.
